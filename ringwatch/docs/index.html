Fraud rings do not look like fraud one transaction at a time.

A ledger hides what a graph shows.

A per-transaction model scores the thirteen rows on the right one at a time. It can score every one of them highly — these are scored 0.9455 to 0.9968 — and still hand a reviewer thirteen separate alerts: thirteen counterparty pairs to chase, with nothing said about how they relate to each other.

Sort by counterparty instead of by amount and the structure appears. The ten accounts form exactly one closed cycle, and the same sum keeps travelling it — $16,435.08 on three separate hops, $16,465.55 on two more. The money is going around the loop rather than through the economy.

That is the difference between an alert and a case. The extraction stage takes a scored edge set and returns the ring itself — its members, their roles, its shape, and the total that moved — which is the unit a reviewer can actually work. Mule networks, layering chains and circular collusion all share that property: the participants are individually unremarkable by construction, because staying unremarkable is the point.

These are real rows from the held-out split, served by GET /rings/ring_00168/explain. Edge risk is the per-transaction scorer's own output. Account identifiers are shown without their bank prefix.

Three stages, and a baseline that gets to win.

RingWatch models 492,163 transactions between 100,572 accounts as a directed multigraph, then runs the same edge set through three stages.

Stage 1

Score the relationships

An edge-featured graph network scores every transaction with its neighbourhood in view, so an account is judged by the company it keeps as well as by its own numbers.

Stage 3

Benchmark it honestly

An XGBoost tabular model is trained on the same time-based split and scored on the same held-out data, under a cost model that prices false positives against missed fraud. It is allowed to beat the graph model, and on two measures it does.

Ring extraction operates on whichever scored edge set it is given. In the artifact shipped here that is the tabular scorer's output — the graph stage is what converts per-edge scores into ring-level structure, not what produced the scores themselves.

Neither model wins outright.

The tabular baseline is the better transaction scorer. It has half again the PR-AUC and it costs $80,355 less to run at its own cost-optimal threshold. Reporting that is not a hedge — a risk product that only shows the chart where it wins has told you nothing about the chart where it loses.

What the graph model buys is recall and structure. It recovers 98.04% of laundering transactions against the baseline's 90.86%, and the community-detection stage that runs on top of a scored edge set is the only part of this system that produces a ring an analyst can open, read, and route for review as a single case.

It cannot act. That is a property of the code, not a policy.

RingWatch is read-only by construction. There is no endpoint that blocks, freezes, holds, or reverses anything, and none can be added without changing what this system is.

Advisory output only

Every response is a score plus an explanation. A flagged ring is a case for a human reviewer to open, not an action taken against an account. Nothing in the serving layer is capable of executing an account or transaction action.

Append-only audit trail

Every score and every ring explanation is written to the audit log — PostgreSQL when DATABASE_URL is set, SQLite or JSONL otherwise — before it is returned to the caller. A flag that was never logged cannot be served.

Reviewable reasoning

GET /rings/{id}/explain returns the accounts and their roles, the contributing transactions with their individual risk scores, the detection method, the model version, and the flag timestamp. The console renders that response verbatim as an audit record.

Ground truth kept visible

Because this runs against a labelled benchmark, the console shows which flagged transactions were actually laundering and which rings matched no known pattern group. The false positives stay on screen.
